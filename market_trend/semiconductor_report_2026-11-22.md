## Latest News & Analyst Commentary

### Sector-Wide

**Historic Selloff — Worst Week in a Quarter Century**
The Philadelphia Semiconductor Index (SOX) finished the week of November 17–20, 2026 with its worst performance in approximately 25 years. The SOXX ETF is now down 34.5% since September 30 — a pace that rivals the worst two-month declines in the fund's history. Market participants cited three converging catalysts: (1) the Nvidia CEO scandal; (2) Google's chip order cuts from Broadcom; and (3) escalating US export control regime targeting advanced AI chips.

**Export Controls Escalate — SE Asia and China**
The US Bureau of Industry and Security (BIS) issued an interim final rule on November 16, taking effect November 19, that extends export licensing requirements to AI chip shipments to Malaysia, Thailand, Indonesia, Singapore, and Brazil — in addition to the pre-existing China restrictions. Nvidia confirmed on its November 17 earnings call that it had halted shipments of certain licensed Hopper-generation AI processors to China. Raymond James analyst Ed Mills warned of a "more restrictive phase" in technology export controls. Jefferies cautioned that the new rules could disrupt AI infrastructure investment across Southeast Asia and accelerate China's push for semiconductor self-reliance.

**Chip Supply Chain Warns of Broader Disruption (Nikkei Asia)**
Per Nikkei, the latest round of US export controls is having cascading effects beyond AI chips — reaching consumer and mobile chip segments and affecting developers based outside of China. The scope is broader than initially expected.

**China Proposes Retaliatory Export Controls**
Beijing's Ministry of Commerce proposed its own export curbs covering 23 AI technologies, including model weights. The public comment period closed November 15; finalization remains pending. This tit-for-tat escalation raises systemic risks for the global semiconductor supply chain.

**BofA: Watching for Earnings Floor**
Bank of America said it is waiting for capital expenditure and earnings estimates to be cut without triggering further stock declines, which would signal a "floor" in the down cycle. The bank is not yet calling a bottom.

**Barron's: Memory Stocks "Agonizingly Cheap"**
Barron's published a note calling memory stocks — primarily MU — "agonizingly cheap" at current levels, though the market has not yet responded positively to the value argument.

## Analysis & Outlook

**A Historic Inflection — The AI Trade Unwinds**

The week ending November 20, 2026 will likely be remembered as the moment the AI semiconductor supercycle officially entered a prolonged correction phase. The sector did not simply decline — it cratered. The SOX index fell 10.73% in a single session, capping a week that wiped out an estimated $2.3 trillion in semiconductor market capitalization. For context, the SOX has now shed approximately 34.5% of its value since September 30 — a two-month decline that rivals the worst stretches of the dot-com bust and the 2020 COVID crash.

Three distinct catalysts converged simultaneously: the forced exit of Nvidia's visionary founder Jensen Huang following a personal scandal with national security implications; a credible report that Google is cutting its Broadcom AI chip orders by 20%; and the US government extending AI chip export restrictions to Southeast Asian countries that had previously served as conduits for China-bound shipments. Any one of these would have caused significant damage. All three together constituted a perfect storm.

**The NVIDIA Void**

The departure of Jensen Huang represents arguably the greatest single leadership risk event in US semiconductor history. Huang was not simply Nvidia's CEO — he was the architect of the company's transformation from a gaming graphics company into the backbone of the global AI infrastructure. No institutional investors, analysts, or hyperscaler partners had modeled a Huang-less Nvidia. The subsequent analyst downgrades — including Truist's brutal price target cut from $346 to $174 — reflect genuine uncertainty about whether Nvidia can maintain its competitive positioning and product roadmap execution without its founder. The simultaneous SEC probe into Nvidia's disclosures compounds governance risk. Interim CEO Colette Kress is a respected CFO but inherits an extraordinarily complex situation: stalled China shipments, an escalating export control environment, and a competitor (AMD) that Wall Street is now actively upgrading as a potential beneficiary of Nvidia's travails.

**Export Controls: A Structural Shift**

The BIS's November 16 interim rule represents a qualitative escalation in US semiconductor policy. By adding licensing requirements for AI chip exports to Malaysia, Thailand, Indonesia, Singapore, and Brazil, the US has effectively closed the Southeast Asia loophole that had allowed advanced chips to reach Chinese end-users through third-party intermediaries. Nvidia's confirmation that it has halted Hopper shipments to China signals that the company sees no short-term path to regaining that market. The near-term revenue impact is significant: China and adjacent markets had represented a meaningful portion of Nvidia's data center revenue. For equipment makers like AMAT, LRCX, and KLAC — whose customers include TSMC, Samsung, and SK Hynix — the export controls create an uncertain capex planning environment.

**Stress Points vs. Value Opportunities**

Not all declines are equal. The EDA software companies — Synopsys (SNPS, -8.06%) and Cadence (CDNS, -7.64%) — are largely insulated from hardware export controls and benefit from the long-term structural growth in chip design complexity. Their declines likely represent fear-driven correlation selling rather than fundamental impairment. Similarly, Texas Instruments (TXN, -7.70%) operates primarily in industrial and automotive analog semiconductors — a market less directly exposed to the AI chip trade war. Microchip Technology (MCHP, -5.18%) and Skyworks Solutions (SWKS, ~-3.93%) were among the smallest decliners, consistent with their lower AI/data center exposure. AMD's simultaneous selloff and analyst upgrades suggest the market is pricing in maximum fear while analysts are identifying the medium-term opportunity: AMD is the clearest near-term beneficiary of Nvidia's difficulties, and an Oppenheimer price target of $600 against a ~$424 close implies significant upside.

**Key Risks and Signals to Watch**

The near-term risk remains to the downside. BofA's posture of "waiting for the earnings floor" suggests institutional investors are not yet convinced that cuts to capex guidance by hyperscalers (Amazon, Microsoft, Google, Meta) have been fully absorbed by consensus estimates. If Q4 2026 earnings season reveals that AI infrastructure spending is moderating — not just rotating — the sector could see a second leg down. On the other hand, if Nvidia can quickly stabilize under Colette Kress's leadership and demonstrate that product roadmap execution is intact, the stock's valuation at current levels could attract value-oriented buyers. Memory stocks (MU in particular) are trading at historically cheap valuations by multiple metrics, and any sign of data center inventory normalization would be a significant catalyst. The geopolitical overhang — particularly whether China retaliates with its own export controls on 23 AI technologies — remains the most unpredictable variable.
