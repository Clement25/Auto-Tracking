## Analysis & Outlook

The week ending September 18, 2026 painted a picture of a semiconductor sector with two very distinct camps: AI-infrastructure leaders posting breathtaking gains, and traditional end-market chipmakers navigating serious headwinds. The theme that defined the week — and arguably the year — is the degree to which AI data-center spending continues to overwhelm every other end-market dynamic.

TSMC's August revenue print of NT$514.8B (US$16.35B) was the clearest signal yet that the AI chip supercycle is not moderating. TSMC publicly stated it cannot meet demand even as it builds new fabs at an unprecedented pace. This "sold out" dynamic flows directly into NVIDIA, which remains the most valuable company on earth at $5.37T, and into Broadcom, whose custom AI silicon (XPU) revenue tripled in a single quarter. Piper Sandler's blanket initiation of AI chip names with "Overweight" ratings — arguing the market is "starving for compute" — reflects the growing analyst consensus that the AI capex wave is supply-constrained, not demand-constrained.

Equipment makers were the standout performers this week. Lam Research and Applied Materials led the sector with gains of 4.69% and ~4%, respectively, both buoyed by record backlogs, AI-driven wafer fab expansion, and rising analyst price targets. LRCX's 26.9% dividend hike is a rare signal of management confidence in the durability of the upcycle — a company does not raise its dividend by that magnitude unless it believes earnings growth is sustainable. The investment implication is clear: wafer fab equipment demand should remain elevated at least through 2027, with Lam, AMAT, KLAC, and ASML as direct beneficiaries.

The sharpest contrast came from Qualcomm, which suffered the worst session of the group at -5.69%. The core problem is structural: Apple's in-house modem is accelerating, reducing QCOM's iPhone share below earlier estimates, while the smartphone market broadly showed weakness with QCOM's handset revenue down 20% YoY. The automotive and IoT chip segments are genuine growth stories, but they are not yet large enough to offset the modem revenue erosion. Skyworks' parallel selloff reinforces the view that the RF/handset supply chain is entering a difficult period. Investors should treat the handset-exposed names (QCOM, SWKS, MCHP) differently from the AI infrastructure plays.

Several stocks to watch closely in the near term: Micron (MU) into its September 30 earnings report, with HBM3E production ramp a critical data point; NVDA heading toward its next earnings cycle, where Blackwell supply constraints and hyperscaler capex trajectory will drive the narrative; and ON Semiconductor, whose post-Investor Day 24% drawdown from its June highs may be creating a re-entry opportunity for investors who believe in the high-voltage SiC/EV power theme. MRVL is also compelling following the Piper Sandler initiation — custom ASICs are the second wave of AI chip demand after Nvidia GPUs, and Marvell sits at the center of that trend.

The macro risk backdrop for semiconductors has improved. Interest rate normalization has reduced discount-rate headwinds on high-multiple names. Geopolitical chip controls remain a watching brief — TSMC's Taiwan operations and ASML's EUV export restrictions are perpetual tail risks — but near-term the sector's earnings power appears to be rising faster than any macro headwind can offset. The IDC forecast of $1.29T in global chip sales for 2026, driven by AI, seems increasingly achievable. For now, the AI supercycle remains the dominant investment thesis, and the data continues to validate it.
